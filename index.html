Loci Line

Loh-sy lie-n

Reading, with a thinking partner

Loci Line is a classroom tool for students and teachers to engage with in class texts, and learn how to use AI to represent their own voice.

Students will walk through and experience a room based on the last chapter the class read, stopping to interact with statues and reflect on themes and opinions they have developed on the text. At the end using the themes they assigned the AI tool in the background will weave a map of all the student opinions, ready to strike up in class discussion or as a piece of art for the classroom wall.

For teachers, you can modify the existing texts’ prompts, or in future be able to add your own, dividing them up as you see fit. This whole experience should take 10-15 minutes and is suggested to be an introduction or reflection activity for your class.

About

Loci Line is a capstone project by Campbell Smith, using Claude code as the engine to create and run the code. Over the 12 week course this prototype has been created with the goal of creating a fun and engaging literacy tool for students year 5 to year 8, and beyond.

The main aim for the app outside of its literacy tool use is to start conversations about AI literacy, and let students safely engage with Large Language Model AI without losing their own voice.

For teachers

This is a prototype. Loci Line is a Media Design capstone project at Te Herenga Waka / Victoria University of Wellington, not a finished product. It isn't connected to any school, and there are no accounts: anything you enter stays in the browser you entered it in. Look around as much as you like, but please don't use it to keep a real class's work yet.

Try the teacher side

Pick one of these made-up rooms to open the dashboard with a sample class already in it, or type your own below.

Add a prompt

Every stop in every room asks one question. You can write over any of them, and your class walks into yours instead of ours — the room, the fork and the record all stay exactly as they are.

The question as written

Tybalt hates the word “peace”. What do you think he’d lose if the fighting stopped?

What you asked instead

We talked about this on Tuesday. Who in the play has the most to lose if everyone calms down?

Open the teacher side It labels your prompts and the walks that come back.

On the teacher side you also read what your class wrote, word for word, and see the artifact each walk made. Nothing there is sent anywhere: prompts and responses stay in the browser they were made in.

What the AI actually does

This is the part most tools are vague about, so here it is as a list. Everything on the left is a choice the student made from a fixed set of options. Nothing on the right is ever opened.

It reads

  • Which way you turned at each fork
  • Which moments you stopped at
  • How long you stayed at each one
  • Which themes you tapped, from five
  • Whether you marked a thought as coming from reading, from class, or from elsewhere

It never reads

  • A single sentence a student writes
  • What they meant by any of it
  • Anything typed into the "where did this come from" box

Student writing is stored, shown back word for word, and printed under the artifact for the class to read aloud. It is never summarised, scored, or sent to a model.

A picture generated from a child's sentences is a machine deciding what those sentences meant. A picture generated from their footsteps is a record of a choice they actually made — and one they can argue with in class.

For parents

The short version: your child types into a box that no AI ever opens, and there is nobody for them to talk to inside this software.

No open chat

There is no conversation with an AI anywhere in the experience. Every input is a button, a tag, or a writing box that stays local to the lesson.

Nothing trains anything

Student writing is not sent to a model, not used for training, and not published. It is shown back to them and to their teacher, word for word.

Nothing is marked

The reading question isn't scored or saved. There are no points, streaks, leaderboards or rewards — deliberately, so it doesn't behave like a game that wants them back.

You can see all of it

The end screen states exactly which pieces of data made the picture. Ask your child to show you theirs; it's designed to be explainable by a ten-year-old.

Where it's up to

All three texts walk: Romeo and Juliet end to end, and the first chapters of Pride and Prejudice and Lord of the Flies as single rooms. The practice wood that teaches the controls is finished, and the teacher's side — writing your own prompts, reading what a class wrote, seeing their artifacts — is working. Student logins and a class roll are next.
